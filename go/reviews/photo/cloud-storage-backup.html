Backblaze — best for set-and-forget backup

Backblaze's whole pitch is simplicity: install it, and it backs up your computer continuously — external drives included. No picking folders, no quotas to babysit. For a photographer with terabytes of RAW files, that “unlimited” framing is exactly the promise you want.

The honest catch: it's backup, not a synced drive. Your files live safely in Backblaze's cloud, but you don't browse them like a Dropbox folder. Restoring is a download or a shipped hard drive — fine for disaster recovery, clumsy for grabbing one file on your phone.

Who it's for: photographers who want the archive protected with zero ongoing decisions.

Who should skip it: anyone who needs their files browsable and shareable like a cloud drive.

pCloud — best buy-once option

pCloud is the rare service that still sells lifetime plans: pay once, keep the storage. It behaves like a proper cloud drive — sync folders, share links, preview files — and offers a client-side encryption add-on (pCloud Crypto) for files you want sealed before they leave your machine. Swiss-based, which matters to privacy-minded buyers.

The honest catch: lifetime means a bigger payment up front, and the encryption add-on costs extra on top. It's a drive, not a backup service — if you delete a synced file everywhere, it's gone everywhere, unless you've set versioning.

Who it's for: photographers who hate subscriptions and want a cloud drive they own outright.

Who should skip it: anyone who wants automatic whole-computer backup rather than a synced folder.

Sync.com — best for privacy-first storage

Sync.com is built around zero-knowledge encryption: your files are encrypted on your device before upload, and the company says it can't read them. Canadian-based with a free tier to try the workflow before paying. For client work under NDAs, that architecture is the selling point.

The honest catch: zero-knowledge means if you lose your password, nobody — including Sync — can recover your files. That's the trade by design. Sharing and collaboration features are thinner than the big consumer drives.

Who it's for: photographers handling sensitive client work who want encryption by architecture, not by promise.

Who should skip it: anyone who has ever forgotten a password and needed support to bail them out.

IDrive — best for multiple devices

IDrive backs up multiple computers, phones, and tablets under one account — PCs, Macs, iPhones, Android. If your archive is spread across a desktop, a laptop, and a phone full of behind-the-scenes shots, one IDrive account covers the fleet instead of one subscription per machine.

The honest catch: first-year pricing is the headline and renewals cost more — read the renewal terms, not the promo banner. The interface is functional rather than beautiful.

Who it's for: photographers with files on many devices who want one backup account for all of them.

Who should skip it: single-computer shooters — you're paying for device seats you won't use.

Buyer questions, answered

Backup vs. cloud drive — which do I need?
Backup (Backblaze, IDrive) copies everything for disaster recovery. A cloud drive (pCloud, Sync.com) syncs files you can browse and share. Most photographers eventually want one of each: a drive for working files, a backup for the archive.

Is one copy in the cloud enough?
No. The old rule still holds: three copies, two different media, one offsite. Cloud backup is the offsite copy — keep a local external drive too.

What about RAW files?
All four services store whatever files you give them — RAW included. The question is workflow: backup services just copy them; drives let you preview and share them.
