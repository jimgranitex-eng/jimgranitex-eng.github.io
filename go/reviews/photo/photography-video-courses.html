KelbyOne — best for serious photographers

KelbyOne is the dedicated photography school: founded by Scott Kelby, taught by working professionals, with a deep catalog across shooting genres and — critically — Lightroom and Photoshop post-processing. Membership unlocks the whole library, new courses land regularly, and members get perks like the Photoshop User community resources. This is where hobbyists go to stop being hobbyists.

The honest catch: it's a subscription, so it rewards steady learners and taxes the curious-but-inconsistent. Some older courses show their age as software moves on — check the publish date on editing tutorials.

Who it's for: photographers committed to the craft who want pro instructors and a real curriculum.

Who should skip it: anyone who just bought their first camera and isn't sure they'll stick with it — start cheaper.

Udemy photography courses — best for one cheap skill

Same marketplace logic as the kitchen side: thousands of individual photo and video courses, bought per-course and kept forever. Portrait lighting, drone video, Premiere basics, Lightroom from zero — pick the exact skill, pay once (usually at a deep sale price), learn at your pace.

The honest catch: anyone can publish, so quality is a spectrum. Filter by high rating count — thousands of ratings at 4.7 beats a perfect 5.0 from eleven people — and check that the course was updated for the current software version.

Who it's for: learners targeting one specific skill without a subscription.

Who should skip it: anyone who wants a guided path from beginner to competent instead of course-hunting.

MasterClass: Annie Leibovitz Teaches Photography — best for the artist's eye

One class, one legend. Annie Leibovitz's MasterClass isn't about camera settings — it's about seeing: portraiture, light, working with subjects, developing a point of view. For a photographer upgrading from phone snapshots, this is the course that teaches you what to point the camera at, which no settings tutorial can do.

The honest catch: it comes with a MasterClass membership, not sold solo — you're buying the platform to get the class. And it won't teach you Lightroom; pair it with a technical course for the full picture.

Who it's for: photographers who have the gear basics down and need vision, not buttons.

Who should skip it: total beginners who still need “what does aperture do” — learn the technical floor first.

Skillshare — best all-rounder subscription

Skillshare is the broad creative subscription: photography, video editing, graphic design, illustration — one membership, thousands of classes, mostly shorter and project-based. If your ambitions span photo and video (and maybe thumbnails, titles, and branding), it's the only option here that covers the whole creator stack.

The honest catch: breadth over depth. Classes are shorter and less rigorous than KelbyOne's — great for getting started on many things, not for mastering one. Instructor quality varies more than the dedicated schools.

Who it's for: creators who want photo, video, and design skills in one subscription.

Who should skip it: photographers who want deep, pro-level mastery of one discipline.

Buyer questions, answered

Can I really learn photography online?
Yes — with one condition: you shoot while you learn. Every course here works if the camera leaves the bag. None of them work as pure viewing.

What about editing — Lightroom or Photoshop?
KelbyOne is the strongest here by design; Udemy has solid per-course options. If editing is your gap, weight those two heaviest.

Free options?
YouTube has excellent free tutorials, but no curriculum and no quality filter. Paid schools buy you structure — that's the actual product.
