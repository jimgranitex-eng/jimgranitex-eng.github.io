Mealime — best for fast weeknight dinners

Mealime's whole design is speed: pick your diet preferences, and it generates a week of quick, healthy meals with an auto-built grocery list. Recipes skew simple and fast — the app knows you're cooking on a Tuesday, not staging a dinner party. Free tier covers the basics; Pro unlocks more.

The honest catch: simplicity is the ceiling too. If you want adventurous cooking or full control over every meal, you'll outgrow it. The free tier is genuinely useful, which also means the upsell to Pro is constant.

Who it's for: busy people who want “what's for dinner” answered in five minutes.

Who should skip it: cooks who plan around their own recipes or want culinary depth.

Eat This Much — best for calorie and macro targets

Eat This Much is the only planner here built around numbers: set a calorie target and macro split, and it auto-generates full days of eating — meals, snacks, grocery list — to hit them. For cutting, bulking, or just eating deliberately, nothing else on this list thinks in macros.

The honest catch: the auto-generated days can feel repetitive and utilitarian — it's optimizing for your numbers, not for joy. You'll want to customize heavily if you actually like food. Free tier is limited; the real planner is Premium.

Who it's for: anyone eating to a target — fitness goals, weight management, structured nutrition.

Who should skip it: intuitive eaters and anyone who finds macro-tracking joyless.

Plan to Eat — best for your own recipe collection

Plan to Eat flips the model: instead of the app choosing your meals, you import and save your recipes — from any site — then drag them onto a calendar. The grocery list builds itself from your plan. It's a planner for people who already know what they like to cook.

The honest catch: it's an organizer, not a recommender. If your recipe box is empty, the app has nothing to work with — there's no “surprise me” engine. The value scales with the collection you bring.

Who it's for: cooks with a beloved recipe stash who need the week organized, not invented.

Who should skip it: anyone hoping the app will tell them what to cook.

eMeals — best done-for-you weekly menus

eMeals writes the menu for you: pick a plan style (keto, family, quick, and many more), and each week brings a complete menu with recipes plus a grocery list that can push straight to online pickup at major grocers. It's the closest thing here to a personal menu-writer.

The honest catch: you're eating their menu, not yours — picky eaters and strong-willed cooks will fight it. It's a subscription for ongoing menus, so the value depends on actually cooking what it sends.

Who it's for: households that want the week's eating fully decided, down to the shopping list.

Who should skip it: anyone who'd rather choose — or import — their own meals.

Buyer questions, answered

Will a meal-planning app actually save me money?
Only if you shop from the list it makes. The savings come from fewer impulse buys and less waste — the app is just the mechanism. An unused subscription saves nothing.

Which one has a real free option?
Mealime and Eat This Much both offer free tiers worth trying before paying. Test the workflow free; subscribe only if you use it weekly.

What happened to PlateJoy?
It shut down in 2025 after years of decline. Older “best meal planning app” lists still recommend it — don't sign up for a dead service. The four above are operating.
